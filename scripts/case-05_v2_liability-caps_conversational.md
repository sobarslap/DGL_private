## Voiceover, clean read (~270 words, about 155 wpm)

> Delivery: chatty and a bit knowing, like a mate who's been stung before. Quick in Act 1, slower on "And it's the supplier who has to prove that. Not you."

Signed a supplier's terms without reading the small print? Yeah. Most of us have.
Somewhere in there is a line like this. It caps what they'll pay you if it all goes wrong.
Here, that's three thousand nine hundred quid. Your loss? Fifty-eight grand.

So let's run it twice. Two firms. Same supplier, same machine, same terms.
Six weeks in, it packs up. Both lose a whole batch. Both get the exact same letter.
One shrugs and takes the three thousand nine hundred. The other pushes back.

Here's the bit most people don't know. A cap like that isn't automatically the end of it.
On their standard terms, it has to be reasonable. And it's the supplier who has to prove that. Not you.
And reasonable is judged on what everyone knew when you signed. Not on the day it broke.
Courts look at who had the clout, and whether you could've gone elsewhere. Whether you knew the clause was there. And how you've dealt before.
Mind you, loads of caps are perfectly fair. They hold up.
But you can only argue what you can actually show.

So, what could each of them show? One had a phone call and a quote lost in someone's inbox. The other had the signed terms, the emails, and eleven past orders.
Same clause. Same supplier. Only one had a case to make.
And honestly? Nobody wants this in court. A small claim takes around forty-one weeks to reach trial.

That's what Dogetlawyer's for. Every supplier, and the cap buried in their terms, where you can see it.
Open one up: which version you signed, when, and the clause flagged. Your paper trail, sorted.

Do one thing this week. Find the liability cap in your three biggest supplier contracts.
Send this to whoever signs your supplier terms.

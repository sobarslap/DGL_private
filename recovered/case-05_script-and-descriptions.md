# Dogetlawyer — Case File № 05
## "Same dispute. Two businesses."

**Format:** side-by-side comparison (new script category — see §7)
**Runtime:** 1:59.0 (119.000 s) · 3,570 frames · 1080 × 1920 · 9:16 · 30 fps
**Audio:** silent AAC track, ready for voiceover
**Subject:** liability caps and exclusion clauses in B2B supplier terms

---

## 1. Why this one is built differently

Cases 01–04 all used the same script category: a single continuous story told
in one voice, hook → problem → law → solution. Four of those in a row start to
blur together, which is the confusion your boss flagged.

Case 05 keeps the same house style, the same length and the same compliance
rules, but changes the **structure of the argument**. Instead of one story, it
runs two in parallel: two businesses in an identical dispute, with one variable
between them. The viewer is not told what the lesson is — they watch the gap
open up and draw it themselves.

Mechanically this shows up as a recurring split-screen motif (scenes 6, 7, 16,
18) and a background field built from two columns, so the format is legible
within the first two seconds.

**S10 · 0:41.4–0:45.8** — UCTA 1977 · s.3 · IT BITES ON WRITTEN STANDARD TERMS. · Where one party deals on the other's written standard terms of business, a clause excluding or restricting liability for their own breach only stands so far as it is reasonable. · SO THE FIRST QUESTION IS: WHOSE TERMS WERE YOU ON?

**S11 · 0:45.8–0:50.6** — UCTA 1977 · s.11(1) · JUDGED ON WHAT WAS KNOWN BACK THEN. · Reasonableness is tested against the circumstances known to, or in the contemplation of, the parties when the contract was made. · NOT ON THE DAY IT WENT WRONG. · Which means the record of that day decides it.

## 5. Voiceover script

Same dispute. Two businesses. Only one kept the record.

This clause is in almost every set of supplier terms. It caps what you can get
back. Here, the cap is three thousand nine hundred pounds. The loss was
fifty-eight thousand. Fifteen times over.

Two firms bought the same system, from the same supplier, on the same terms.
Six weeks in, it failed. Both of them scrapped a full batch.

This is everything the first one could produce. A phone call. A quote in
someone's inbox. The second had the signed order, and the version.

The supplier wrote to both of them. Word for word, the same letter. One
accepted it.

Here's what most people don't know. A cap isn't a wall. It's a claim.

Under the Unfair Contract Terms Act, it's for the one relying on the clause to
show it was reasonable. That test applies to written standard terms. So the
first question is whose terms you were on.

And it's judged on what was known when the contract was made. Not on the day it
broke.

For a cap set in money, two more questions. What could they expect to have, to
pay it? And could they have insured?

The schedule adds four more. Bargaining strength. Alternatives. What you knew.
And your history together.

Two things no clause can ever reach. Death or injury from negligence. And
title, on a sale of goods.

Plenty of caps are perfectly reasonable. You only find out which kind you have
if you can show the facts.

This is what each of them could put forward. Same clause. Only one of them had
an argument.

And if it goes as far as a hearing, a small claim takes about forty-one weeks.
The bigger track, nearly fifty-six.

One of them took the three thousand nine hundred. The other settled well above
it. Nothing about the clause was different.

The difference wasn't the clause. It was the file.

That's what Dogetlawyer keeps. Every contract, and the cap inside it. Every
supplier, with its limit pulled out. What it's based on, and how much. The one
that matters is the one nobody has read.

Open it, and there's the record. Which version. Sent when. Signed when. And the
clause, flagged.

Eleven orders on the same terms. All of them dated. That is a course of
dealing.

Find your caps before you need them. Start with your three biggest suppliers.

Send this to whoever signs your supplier terms.

## 6. Sources and compliance

### Law

Every legal statement on frame is drawn from primary legislation, read directly
on legislation.gov.uk.

| On screen | Source |
|---|---|
| Burden of proof is on the party relying on the clause | Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, **s.11(5)** |
| Applies where one party deals on the other's written standard terms | UCTA 1977, **s.3(1)–(2)** |
| Reasonableness judged on circumstances known when the contract was made | UCTA 1977, **s.11(1)** |
| For a money cap: resources available, and availability of insurance | UCTA 1977, **s.11(4)(a)–(b)** |
| Bargaining strength · alternatives · knowledge of the term · previous course of dealing | UCTA 1977, **Schedule 2(a)–(c)** |
| Death or personal injury from negligence cannot be excluded at all | UCTA 1977, **s.2(1)** |
| Title on a sale of goods cannot be excluded or restricted at all | UCTA 1977, **s.6(1)** |

Scene 15 states plainly that many caps are reasonable and do hold, and the
frame carries *GENERAL POSITION — OUTCOMES TURN ON THE FACTS*. The video does
not tell anyone their clause is unenforceable.

### Statistics

### Constraints held

- All figures, messages, clauses and business names are fictional demo examples. No real client or bank data. Scenes carrying demo content are labelled FICTIONAL DEMO EXAMPLE on frame, and the closing card repeats it.
- No prices, plans or offers anywhere.
- No implication of payment recovery, or of bank or email sync.
- Closing card carries the constant fine print: *Contract management software for UK small businesses — not a substitute for legal advice.*
- British business names throughout (Harlow Bridge Joinery, Caldew Fabrication, Mereton Systems, Brayfield Plant Hire, Ivelet Logistics).
